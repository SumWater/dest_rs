# 5. Results

## 5.1 Main Results: Control-Quality Trade-off

Table~\ref{tab:main} presents the primary comparison across different PEFT configurations. We report valid perplexity (PPL) and 9-class strategy accuracy, evaluated via swap-sample generation (30 validation contexts $\times$ 9 strategies = 270 generations per experiment). For the four key experiments (B3, B4, B7, B9), we report means and standard deviations across three independent training seeds (42, 43, 44).

**Text instruction baseline.** The frozen Qwen3-8B model with explicit strategy text in the prompt (``As the next speaker, use the following negotiation strategy: \{strategy\}'') achieves 27.41\% strategy accuracy. This confirms that contemporary LLMs possess some zero-shot strategy awareness, though the control is far from reliable. The model frequently generates meta-instructions or refuses to follow the strategy prompt, resulting in inconsistent behavior.

**LoRA with strategy text.** Training only LoRA (r=16, $\alpha$=32) with the same strategy text prompt yields 42.22\% accuracy and 7.81 PPL. This serves as a fair LoRA baseline—unlike earlier work that compared Prefix against LoRA without strategy input, our LoRA baseline receives the same strategy information as Prefix. While LoRA substantially improves generation fluency (PPL reduction), its strategy accuracy remains well below Prefix-only.

**Prefix-only achieves strongest controllability.** The strategy-specific Prefix bank (20 virtual tokens per strategy, trained with generation loss only) reaches 55.87\% accuracy (95\% CI [52.41, 59.33]) across three seeds, with a standard deviation of only 1.28 percentage points. This makes Prefix-only the strongest single-branch strategy control baseline in our setting. However, it comes at the cost of generation quality: valid PPL of 10.54 is substantially higher than all LoRA-augmented variants.

**Prefix+LoRA joint training introduces control-quality trade-off.** When Prefix and LoRA are trained jointly (B4), PPL drops to 7.93—a 24.7\% improvement over Prefix-only. However, strategy accuracy falls to 37.20\% (95\% CI [33.87, 40.54]), a decline of approximately 19 percentage points. The non-overlapping confidence intervals between B3 and B4 confirm that this trade-off is statistically robust. LoRA clearly improves language modeling adaptation, but its shared parameters interfere with the strategy-specific steering signal learned by individual Prefix embeddings.

**Warm-start and staged training provide modest improvements.** Initializing with a pre-trained Prefix from B3 and jointly training for one epoch (B7) yields 40.49\% accuracy (95\% CI [37.04, 43.83]). Freezing the B3-trained Prefix and training only LoRA (B9) yields 40.00\% accuracy (95\% CI [36.54, 43.33]). While both variants slightly outperform B4 (37.20\%), their confidence intervals substantially overlap with B4's, indicating that the improvement magnitude is modest and not statistically significant in our current evaluation setup. The PPL of B7 (8.18) and B9 (8.07) also remains comparable to B4 (7.93), confirming that these variants maintain LoRA-level generation quality while partially preserving Prefix controllability.

Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} visualizes this trade-off: Prefix-only occupies the high-accuracy, high-PPL region, while all LoRA-augmented variants cluster in the low-PPL, moderate-accuracy region. No configuration simultaneously achieves the controllability of Prefix-only and the fluency of LoRA-augmented models.

## 5.2 Per-Class Strategy Breakdown

Figure~\ref{fig:perclass} shows per-class accuracy for B3 (Prefix-only) and B4 (Prefix+LoRA), alongside the evaluator calibration accuracy on human-written responses.

Several patterns emerge. First, the strategies most affected by LoRA are *other-need*, *uv-part*, and *showing-empathy*—these drop by 7--70 percentage points when LoRA is added. This is consistent with the observation that these are relatively rare and semantically subtle strategies; LoRA's dominant generation patterns may override the finer-grained Prefix signals for these classes. In contrast, *small-talk* and *vouch-fair* are relatively preserved, and *self-need* actually shows similar accuracy between B3 and B4.

Second, the evaluator calibration accuracy (gray bars) varies dramatically across strategies, from 84.2\% (*elicit-pref*) to 0.0\% (*no-need*). The fact that model-generated *no-need* achieves 93.3\% accuracy despite the evaluator being unable to recognize human-written *no-need* strongly suggests shortcut learning: the model generates exaggerated refusal patterns that the evaluator associates with *no-need*, even though these patterns do not reflect natural strategy usage. This finding underscores the importance of evaluator calibration when interpreting automatic strategy accuracy.

## 5.3 Diagnostic Variants: Why Simple Interventions Fail

Table~\ref{tab:diag} reports results from a series of diagnostic experiments designed to isolate the source of Prefix-LoRA interference.

**Orthogonal regularization.** Adding a local-global orthogonal constraint between Prefix-induced and LoRA-induced hidden-state deltas (B5; $\lambda_{\text{orth}}=0.05$) does not improve strategy control (36.06\%) and in fact performs worse than B4. Increasing the orth weight to $\lambda_{\text{orth}}=1.0$ (B6$_{\text{orth}=1.0}$) yields 40.37\% accuracy, a marginal improvement over B4 but still far below B3. Notably, the orth loss rapidly decays to near-zero values during training, suggesting that the model discovers geometric configurations that minimize the cosine similarity penalty without achieving functional disentanglement—geometric orthogonality does not imply functional separation.

**Auxiliary strategy classification.** Adding a strategy classification head on top of the Prefix-induced hidden delta (B6$_{\text{fix}}$; $\lambda_{\text{cls}}=1.0$) yields only 31.85\% accuracy, substantially worse than B4. Increasing $\lambda_{\text{cls}}$ to 5.0 improves accuracy to 39.63\%, but still below B4. The classification loss converges rapidly (approaching zero within one epoch), indicating that the classifier learns to distinguish strategies from internal representations. However, this internal separability does not translate into strategy-consistent generation: the classifier operates on hidden states and provides only a scalar supervision signal per sample, whereas the generation loss distributes its signal across hundreds of tokens per sample.

**Gradient routing.** To test whether gradient conflict between generation and classification losses is the primary interference mechanism, we route the generation loss gradient exclusively to LoRA and the classification loss gradient exclusively to Prefix (B6$_{\text{grad}}$). This yields 37.78\% accuracy, an improvement over B6$_{\text{fix}}$ (31.85\%) but still below B4 (37.20\% in the same experimental group). This suggests that gradient conflict explains part of the interference, but not all of it. Removing orthogonal regularization from the gradient routing setup (B6$_{\text{grad\_no\_orth}}$) causes training to diverge, indicating that orth loss serves as a necessary coordination signal rather than a sufficient decoupling mechanism.

**Contrastive generation loss.** Motivated by the sparsity of the classification signal, we introduce a contrastive generation objective: the model's generation loss using the correct strategy Prefix should be lower than using a randomly sampled incorrect strategy Prefix. However, the contrastive loss remains at zero throughout training for both the orth-augmented (B6$_{\text{contrastive}}$, 42.59\%) and orth-free (B6$_{\text{contrastive\_no\_orth}}$, 41.11\%) variants. This indicates that replacing the target Prefix with an incorrect one does not appreciably increase the token-level generation loss—the majority of generated tokens are strategy-agnostic, and the standard autoregressive LM loss is insufficiently sensitive to strategy-level differences.

**Frozen Prefix analysis.** To further isolate the interference mechanism, we freeze the B3-trained Prefix and train only LoRA (the B9 variant in the diagnostic group, achieving 44.44\% accuracy). Even though the Prefix weights are identical to B3, strategy accuracy drops from 55.93\% to 44.44\% when LoRA is introduced at inference time. This provides direct evidence that LoRA changes the operating environment of Prefix: the strategy signals encoded in Prefix embeddings were learned in a "clean" base model attention space, and the LoRA-modified Q/K/V/O projections partially disrupt these signals at inference time.

## 5.4 Evaluator Calibration and Reliability

Table~\ref{tab:calib} reports the LLM-based strategy evaluator's accuracy on human-written validation responses. The overall calibration accuracy is 52.0\%, revealing substantial ambiguity in the strategy classification task itself. Several strategies exhibit particularly poor evaluator reliability: *no-need* (0.0\%), *uv-part* (25.0\%), and *other-need* (30.0\%). The evaluator also shows systematic bias toward *self-need*, classifying approximately 36--38\% of inputs as *self-need* regardless of content.

These findings have important implications for interpreting automatic strategy accuracy. First, overall accuracy should not be interpreted as absolute strategy controllability—a 52\% calibration ceiling means that nearly half of the classification variance may stem from evaluator limitations rather than model behavior. Second, per-class accuracy must be interpreted in light of calibration: strategies like *other-need* and *uv-part* are nearly indistinguishable by the evaluator, so their low model accuracy may reflect evaluator noise rather than poor control. Third, the *no-need* case exemplifies a dangerous shortcut: the model generates exaggerated rejection patterns that the evaluator classifies as *no-need*, while the evaluator fails to recognize genuine human *no-need* utterances.

We therefore recommend that all LLM-as-judge strategy evaluation studies include evaluator calibration, per-class analysis, and—where feasible—small-scale human evaluation for sanity checking. Our calibration data and methodology are publicly available.

## 5.5 Multi-Seed Stability Analysis

To assess the robustness of our core findings, we trained B3, B4, B7, and B9 across three random seeds (42, 43, 44). The standard deviation of strategy accuracy is 1.28pp for B3, 3.78pp for B4, 2.19pp for B7, and 2.98pp for B9.

Two conclusions emerge with high confidence. First, the control-quality trade-off between B3 and B4 is stable across seeds: B3 consistently achieves the highest strategy accuracy (54.3--57.4\%) and B4 consistently achieves the lowest among PEFT methods (32.6--41.9\%), with non-overlapping per-seed distributions. Second, PPL is highly stable across seeds for all configurations: B3 PPL ranges from 10.40 to 10.65, while B4/B7/B9 PPL ranges from 7.76 to 8.48, confirming that LoRA reliably improves generation adaptation.

The relative ordering of B4, B7, and B9 is less stable across seeds. In seed 42, B7 outperforms B4 by 4.7pp; in seed 43, B7 outperforms B4 by 4.8pp; but in seed 44, B4 outperforms B7 by 0.4pp. This variability, combined with overlapping bootstrap confidence intervals, suggests that the advantage of warm-start and staged training over simple joint training is modest and seed-dependent. Future work with larger evaluation sets and more seeds is needed to establish whether these variants provide statistically reliable improvements.
